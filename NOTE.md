# Why "Situationship" meets the judge's five criteria

Entry: **Situationship** (13 characters, one word, no trailing newline).

The judge weighs, most important first:

1. **On-subject fit — one word describing Wi-Fi as the entrant lives it.**
   The word names the relationship everyone actually has with home Wi-Fi:
   it is there, it is not there, it never commits. The entry is about the
   lived experience of Wi-Fi itself, not about routers, passwords, or the
   internet in general.

2. **Funny at once — a real laugh, not a pun that needs explaining.**
   The joke lands the instant the word is read: your Wi-Fi is in a
   situationship with you. It texts back sometimes and ghosts you the rest
   of the time. No setup, no wordplay to decode, no footnote required.

3. **True — recognisable from how home Wi-Fi actually behaves.**
   Everyone has watched full bars collapse to one bar mid-call, a video
   freeze at the best part, a connection that works in the kitchen and
   dies in the bedroom. Hot and cold, never official, is exactly how that
   feels — the reader recognises their own Wi-Fi in it.

4. **Fresh — avoids the words everyone reaches for.**
   No "slow", no "buffering", no "loading", no ghosting (already crowded
   in this field). Dating-slang applied to a router is a surprising angle
   on the most familiar annoyance there is: the connection that will not
   commit.

5. **Works as a single word, spelled so a reader gets it instantly.**
   One common English word, 13 letters, standard spelling, no hyphen, no
   camel-case mash-up, no proper noun, no explanation needed. It stands
   alone as the name of a network: "Situationship" reads as a Wi-Fi name
   on its own.
